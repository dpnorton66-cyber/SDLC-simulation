Fixed assumptions?

20 features, each 6.5 person-days (analysis 1, design 1, build 3, test 1.5). Team of four. Sprints are ten days. Fixing a feature that was wrong costs the effort already spent on it, growing with the time since it was specified (up to three times).

What to look for

  1. When value arrives. Waterfall delivers nothing until the end, then everything at once. Continuous starts delivering in the first week. Time to first value is the clearest single difference.
  2. The cost of being wrong late. Every lifecycle has the same wrong features. What differs is when they are found and how much has been built on them by then. Watch the size of the red rework band at the end of a waterfall run.
  3. Spent before feedback. This is the money at risk. Waterfall spends the whole budget before anyone outside the team sees the product.
  4. Change during the project. Raise the changes-while-you-build setting. The longer a feature waits between being specified and being released, the more likely the world has moved. Long lifecycles suffer most.
  5. Release cost is the price of continuous delivery. Set the cost of a release to 15 and continuous becomes the slowest and most expensive option, keeping only its early first value. Set it to 0 and it wins on almost every measure. Reducing release cost is what the investment in automation buys, and without it small batches do not pay.
  6. Incremental and agile differ. Incremental is scope-boxed: five features, phase by phase, however long they take. Agile is time-boxed: ten days, whatever fits, with work flowing inside the sprint. Watch the activity strip: agile releases on a fixed cadence and pulls fixes into the very next sprint, but the team can sit idle at the end of a sprint when nothing else fits. That idle time is the price of the timebox; continuous removes it by removing the box.
