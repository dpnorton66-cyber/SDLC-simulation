Quality and value practices?

How many defects are introduced, unintentionally, for every 10 person-days of build. What then happens to each defect:

  • Testing finds it. Fixing costs one day, rising to three if the test comes long after the build.
  • About one in seven (15%) slips past testing and reaches users, matching the published average of 85% removed before delivery. That counts as a failed release and is fixed afterwards.
  • Releasing many items together adds integration work, which grows with the square of the number of items.

Share of wrong items caught at a demo of the working item before release. A design review catches half that share, because a document is harder to judge.

Item values follow a steep curve: the top fifth of items carry about three quarters of the value, in line with published feature-usage data. Items are started in estimated-value order whenever the lifecycle allows a choice, and every release improves the estimate by a fifth.

Below 100%, no new items are started once delivered value reaches the target. Waterfall cannot stop early because nothing is released until the end.

Model assumptions
  1. Scope. 260 person-days, cut into items of equal size. Each item is 15% analysis, 15% design, 47% build and 23% test.
  2. Team. Seven people, each giving one day of effort per day. The Scrum Guide's guidance is ten or fewer; the common convention is seven plus or minus two. A work task absorbs at most one person-day per day; a release can absorb the whole team. A person whose task needs less than a day moves on to the next task.
  3. Wrong from the start. Each item is given a rank, evenly spaced and in random order. When an item is specified it is wrong if its rank is below the current chance of being wrong. With no learning this makes exactly the stated share wrong, and which items is random. The error is invisible until the item is released.
  4. Learning from feedback. Each misunderstood item that users expose multiplies the chance that a not-yet-specified item is wrong by (1 − the learning setting). Items already specified are unaffected. An item that was right when specified and changed later teaches nothing about specifying, and changes in the world are not reduced by learning. The rule is identical for every lifecycle.
  5. Changes while you build. Once an item is specified (analysis complete), the number of days until its requirement changes is drawn from a geometric distribution with the stated monthly rate spread over 21 working days. An item changes at most once. A change after the item is finished and correct has no effect. Capers Jones reports requirements creep during development from under 1% to over 10% per calendar month; the default of 3% sits in the middle of that range.
  6. Discovery. A wrong item is found only when users see it. Nothing in analysis, design, build or test finds it. This is deliberate: the model isolates the effect of feedback timing and does not simulate reviews, prototypes or defects.
  7. Rework. Fixing a wrong item costs 60% of the original effort spent on it so far (not counting earlier fixes), multiplied by 1 + (days since specification ÷ 50), capped at 3. This is a cost-of-change curve: some of the work survives, and the longer the mistake stands the more has been built on it. Boehm's phase data shows fix cost rising by roughly 1.2, 1.25, 1.75 and 3 times across successive phases; Boehm and Basili (2001) put the after-delivery escalation at up to 100 times for large systems but nearer 5 to 1 for small ones. The cap of 3 follows the phase data and the small-system finding.
  8. Value delivered. An item delivers its value only when it is released and correct. A wrong item delivers nothing until fixed. An escaped defect does not remove value.
  9. Cadence. Agile sprints are ten working days: the Scrum Guide caps sprints at one month and about 58% of teams use two weeks. Incremental increments are sized to the chosen length (default four weeks) from team capacity; DSDM puts timeboxes at two to four weeks with six as the exception, and SAFe program increments at 8 to 12 weeks, so four to six weeks is typical for a release-level increment. Continuous releases each item when ready, which is deployment on demand at day resolution. Waterfall releases once.
  10. Releases. Each costs the stated fixed effort. Waterfall releases once, then once more for fixes. Incremental releases each increment, with fixes released separately before the next. Agile releases whatever is finished at the end of every sprint. Continuous releases each item and each fix on its own.
  11. Agile planning. Sprint capacity is 70 person-days less the release cost. Fixes are planned first, then items in backlog order while they fit. Unfinished work carries over. Nothing new starts mid-sprint.
  12. Defects. Build introduces defects at the stated rate per 10 person-days of build. Testing finds them; each costs one person-day multiplied by 1 + (days between build and test ÷ 20), capped at 3. One in five defects escapes testing. At each release the number that actually reach users is a random draw (Poisson) around that expected number; severe ones (one in five) trigger a hotfix release in every lifecycle, outside any sprint or increment cadence, and count the release as failed; minor ones are fixed under the same cost rule and go out with the next release. Integration at a release costs 0.02 × rate × n(n − 1) ÷ 2 person-days for n items, so it grows with the square of the batch. The 15% escape share matches Capers Jones's US average of 85% defect removal efficiency; the default rate of 2 puts rework and defect cost together at roughly a third of effort in the typical scenario, below the 40 to 50% Jones and McConnell report for industry, so the model is if anything generous.
  13. Reviews. When an item's design is complete, a product owner review catches it if it is wrong with probability half the review setting. When its test is complete, a demo of the working item catches it with probability equal to the setting. A caught item is fixed before release under the rework rule, counts as a lesson if it was misunderstood, and does not count as a failed release. Jones's data has formal inspections finding 60 to 65% of latent defects and informal reviews under 50%; the default of 40% for a demo is conservative.
  14. Value. Item values follow a power curve (value proportional to 1 ÷ rank1.2), assigned to items at random; the top fifth of items carry about three quarters of the value and the top eighth about two thirds. Pendo's 2019 usage data found 12% of features generating 80% of use; the older Standish figure of 64% of features rarely or never used rests on four applications and is treated as supporting evidence only. The team's estimate is the true value with lognormal noise at the chosen level, which shrinks by a fifth with every release. Whenever a lifecycle chooses what to start next (a slice, a sprint, the next item) it takes the highest estimated value. Waterfall's order does not matter because everything is released together.
  15. Stop rule. If a target below 100% is set, no new items are started once delivered value reaches it; items in progress and fixes are completed. A run finishes when every started item is live and correct.
  16. Not modelled. Dependencies between items, absences, estimation error, hand-off queues between phases, sprint ceremony time, and release cost falling as teams automate.
  17. Sources. Scrum Guide (2020); State of Agile survey data on sprint length; DSDM Agile Project Framework; Scaled Agile Framework, Program Increment; DORA State of DevOps Report 2024; Capers Jones, Software Engineering Best Practices (2010) and The Economics of Software Quality (2011); Boehm, Software Engineering Economics (1981); Boehm and Basili, Software Defect Reduction Top 10 List, IEEE Computer (2001); McConnell, Code Complete; Pendo, 2019 Feature Adoption Report; Standish Group via Johnson, XP 2002.
  18. DORA measures. Deployment frequency is days elapsed divided by releases. Lead time is from first work on an item to its first release. Change failure rate is releases containing at least one wrong item divided by releases. The item-level rate is items wrong at first release divided by items released. Time to restore is from the release that exposed an item to the release of its fix. Change failure rate follows DORA's definition: releases that needed a hotfix or rollback, which here means releases carrying at least one severe escaped defect. Capers Jones's data puts about 1% of delivered defects as catastrophic and about 20% as major, so one in five escaped defects is treated as severe; the rest are fixed in the next release without counting as a failure. Releases carrying a wrong requirement are reported beside it but are not DORA failures. Time to restore counts severe defects only. Rework rate, which DORA added in 2024, is releases that carried a fix divided by releases. DORA's 2024 benchmarks: elite teams deploy on demand with lead time under a day, change failure rate near 5% and recovery under an hour; low performers deploy less than monthly. DORA's 2024 bands for change failure rate were about 5% elite, 20% high, 10% medium and 40% low; DORA itself notes the measure has long been the outlier among its four.
  19. Randomness. The draw of wrong items and change days is fixed by the draw number and the settings, and is identical for every lifecycle.

What to look for

  1. When value arrives. Waterfall delivers nothing until the end, then everything at once. Continuous starts delivering in the first week. Time to first value is the clearest single difference.
  2. The cost of being wrong late. Every lifecycle has the same wrong features. What differs is when they are found and how much has been built on them by then. Watch the size of the red rework band at the end of a waterfall run.
  3. Spent before feedback. This is the money at risk. Waterfall spends the whole budget before anyone outside the team sees the product.
  4. Defects follow batch size and delay. Waterfall pays most for defects even at the same defect rate: it tests long after building, and it integrates everything at once, where the cost grows with the square of the batch. Set defects to 2 and compare the defect cost column. This is the defect-density difference between lifecycles, and it comes from timing, not from better programmers.
  5. Stopping when good enough. Set the stop target to 80%. Agile and continuous build about three quarters of the items and finish with 90% of the value; waterfall and incremental cannot use the rule, because they commit to scope before value is visible. This is where most of the difference in project success rates comes from: fixed time and variable scope, against fixed scope and variable time.
  6. Learning only helps if it arrives in time. Set learning to 25% and run waterfall: nothing changes, because every item was specified before the first lesson arrived. Run continuous and watch the chance of a wrong item fall under the feature grid as each release teaches the team something. Agile and incremental sit between. This is the product owner effect: feedback is only worth what you can still act on.
  7. Change during the project. At published rates of a few per cent a month, change matters less than misunderstanding on a three-month project; raise it to 10% and the long lifecycles suffer most, because their items wait longest between being specified and being released.
  8. Release cost is the price of continuous delivery. Set the cost of a release to 15 and continuous becomes the slowest and most expensive option, keeping only its early first value. Set it to 0 and it wins on almost every measure. Reducing release cost is what the investment in automation buys, and without it small batches do not pay.
  9. Item size only helps when items are released small. Set backlog items to very small and run waterfall: nothing improves, because the items are still released together. Run continuous with the same items and first value arrives on day 2 with a lead time of a few days, but 100 releases at 2 person-days each is 200 person-days of overhead. Small items and cheap releases go together. Then set items to very large: continuous starts to behave like incremental, and agile struggles because an item bigger than a sprint cannot fit in one.
  10. Incremental and agile differ. Incremental is scope-boxed: an increment's worth of items, phase by phase, however long it takes, and at a typical four to six weeks an increment is two to three sprints' worth of work released at once. Agile is time-boxed: ten days, whatever fits, with work flowing inside the sprint. Watch the activity strip: agile releases on a fixed cadence and pulls fixes into the very next sprint, but the team can sit idle at the end of a sprint when nothing else fits. That idle time is the price of the timebox; continuous removes it by removing the box.
